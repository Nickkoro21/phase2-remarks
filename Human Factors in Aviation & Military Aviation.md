# Human Factors in Aviation & Military Aviation: A Squadron Stand-Down Brief for the T-6A Texan II Training Community

## 1. Executive Summary

Modern aviation safety is a story about systems, not individuals. The single most important shift in human factors thinking over the past forty years is the move from blaming the pilot at the end of the chain to interrogating the organization that built the chain. Two accidents anchor that shift more cleanly than any others. On 24 June 1994, B-52H **Czar 52** rolled past 90 degrees of bank at 250 feet AGL at Fairchild Air Force Base and killed all four aircrew — the textbook organizational accident, a known "rogue" pilot tolerated for years up a chain of command that knew exactly what he was doing. On 19 July 1989, **United 232** lost all three hydraulic systems over Iowa after an uncontained fan-disk failure, and a crew that had never trained for the scenario flew the airplane to a survivable crash with differential thrust, saving 184 of 296 souls. One accident was made inevitable by an organization that refused to act; the other was made survivable by an organization that had trained its crews to think together. That is the central paradox of this brief: the same human factors discipline explains both.

For instructor pilots and advanced students in a T-6A trainer squadron, the lessons compress into five hard takeaways:

1. **"Pilot error" is a verdict, not an explanation.** Czar 52 was not a Bud Holland accident; it was a 92nd Bomb Wing accident. If your squadron tolerates a "hot stick" who routinely busts bank limits, altitude floors, or briefed profiles, you are pre-staging your own Class A. Tier 3 (Supervisory) and Tier 4 (Organizational) factors in DoD HFACS 7.0 do not appear at the moment of impact — they appear in the months and years before.
2. **CRM is behavior, not vocabulary.** UA232's crew did not "communicate well." They executed identifiable, trainable behaviors — workload distribution, distributed situational awareness, inquiry/advocacy, leadership/followership — under conditions for which no checklist existed. Those behaviors transfer to an IP/SP cockpit on a low-level route as cleanly as they did to a DC-10 over Iowa.
3. **Fatigue is a physiological state, not a willpower problem.** Seventeen hours of continuous wakefulness produces cognitive performance equivalent to a 0.05% blood-alcohol concentration; 24 hours equals roughly 0.10% (Dawson & Reid, *Nature* 388:235, 1997). Back-to-back IP sorties and predawn show times put trainer squadrons inside this envelope on a routine basis.
4. **Normalization of deviance is invisible to those drifting.** Czar 52 (1994), C-17 Elmendorf (2010), Challenger (1986), and Columbia (2003) all share the same mechanism — an organization gradually accepted boundary violations until the violation became the standard. The Diane Vaughan signal lights are simple: *"we've always done it this way," "no one got hurt last time," "he's our best pilot."*
5. **The single most important supervisory act in your career may be one uncomfortable conversation.** Lt Col Mark McGeehan tried to ground Bud Holland and was overruled. He died in the right seat trying to protect his squadron. Supervisory courage is not optional in this profession; it is the failure mode that defines or prevents the next Class A.

The remainder of this document develops the historical evolution of the field, six interlocking theoretical frameworks each tied to a specific accident, the two anchor case studies in depth, comparative military and civilian cases, deep dives into fatigue and drift, future trends, and concrete squadron-level recommendations.

## 2. The Problem Space — Historical Evolution

The field has moved through five rough phases. None has fully replaced the one before it; the sediment accumulates, and modern accident boards work in all five layers simultaneously.

**1903–1950s — The stick-and-rudder era.** Investigations terminated at "pilot error." Aircraft were unreliable, crew training was thin, and the dominant question was whether the pilot did or did not do what the book said. The era produced excellent airmen and almost no usable systemic insight.

**1960s–1970s — Ergonomics and SHELL.** As jets became reliable, the residual accident rate exposed the cockpit as a system, not a tool. Frank Hawkins and Elwyn Edwards developed the SHELL model (Software–Hardware–Environment–Liveware, centered on Liveware) to describe pilot interactions with the rest of the system. The anchor accident is **Eastern Air Lines 401** (L-1011 N310EA, Everglades, 29 December 1972). A burnt-out nose-gear indicator bulb captured the attention of all three flight deck crew; meanwhile the autopilot dropped from altitude hold into Control Wheel Steering when someone bumped the yoke. The L-1011 descended into the swamp from 2,000 feet, killing 101 of 176 on board (NTSB AAR-73-14). The accident is the textbook L–Software / L–Hardware interface failure: a pilot population that did not understand the autopilot's reversion logic, an altitude alert chime located under the flight engineer's workstation (the seat he had vacated to check the gear visually), and an attention bottleneck that no amount of stick-and-rudder skill could fix.

**1977 — Tenerife.** On 27 March 1977, KLM 4805 and Pan Am 1736 collided on the runway at Los Rodeos in fog, killing 583 — still the deadliest aviation accident in history. The Spanish, Dutch, and US investigations identified a stack of holes: a bomb diversion that crowded a regional field, fog that closed visibility below taxi minimums, no ground radar, non-standard ATC and crew phraseology (the KLM first officer's "we are now at takeoff" and the controller's "OK"), simultaneous transmissions that produced a heterodyne squeal masking Pan Am's "we're still taxiing down the runway" call, and a steep authority gradient on the KLM flight deck where Captain Veldhuyzen van Zanten — KLM's chief 747 instructor, whose face was in the company magazine — overrode his flight engineer's question "is he not clear, that Pan American?" with an emphatic "oh, yes" and continued the takeoff roll. Tenerife is the canonical Swiss Cheese accident and the catalyst that put cockpit communication and authority gradient on the industry's agenda.

**1978 — United 173 Portland.** On 28 December 1978 a United DC-8 ran out of fuel while holding to troubleshoot a landing gear problem, crashing into a suburban Portland neighborhood and killing 10. The captain had locked onto the gear problem for over an hour; the first officer and flight engineer hinted at the fuel state and were not heard. NTSB Air Safety Investigator and aviation psychologist Alan Diehl's recommendation triggered NASA's June 1979 Resource Management on the Flight Deck workshop — the founding event of Cockpit (later Crew) Resource Management. United Airlines launched the industry's first full CRM program in 1981.

**1986 — Challenger and the introduction of normalization of deviance.** Diane Vaughan's 1996 book *The Challenger Launch Decision* analyzed the 1986 loss of Space Shuttle Challenger and coined "normalization of deviance" — the gradual process by which "people within the organization become so much accustomed to a deviation that they don't consider it as deviant, despite the fact that they far exceed their own rules for the elementary safety." Solid rocket booster O-ring erosion had been observed flight after flight; each non-catastrophic outcome reset the baseline of what counted as "acceptable." The same mechanism reappeared with foam shedding from Columbia's external tank in 2003.

**1990 — Reason publishes *Human Error*.** James Reason's Swiss Cheese Model gave the industry a vocabulary for active failures (the unsafe act at the sharp end) and latent failures (organizational pathogens that lie dormant until aligned with a triggering event). It is impossible to overstate how completely this model now dominates accident analysis across aviation, healthcare, nuclear power, and process industries.

**1994 — Czar 52.** The B-52H crash at Fairchild AFB became the textbook organizational accident — used in USAF safety courses, civilian CRM curricula, and graduate-level leadership texts (Tony Kern's *Darker Shades of Blue*, 1995). Section 4 develops it in depth.

**1999–2003 — HFACS and TEM mature.** Shappell and Wiegmann published the Human Factors Analysis and Classification System (FAA report DOT/FAA/AM-00/7, 2000), a four-tier taxonomy — Acts, Preconditions, Supervision, Organizational Influences — operationalizing Reason's model for routine use by accident boards. DoD adopted HFACS service-wide; **DoD HFACS 7.0** is the version codified in current Air Force Safety Automated System (AFSAS) practice for Class A, B, and physiological mishaps under AFI/DAFI 91-204. In parallel, Helmreich, Klinect, and Wilhelm formalized Threat and Error Management (TEM) out of the Line Operations Safety Audit (LOSA) program at the University of Texas, recasting CRM around an operational triad: threats → errors → undesired aircraft states (UAS), each managed by countermeasures.

**2009 — Air France 447 and the automation problem comes to the front.** On 1 June 2009 an A330 crew lost pitot indications in cruise over the Atlantic Intertropical Convergence Zone, the autopilot disconnected, and the pilot flying made sustained nose-up sidestick inputs that put the aircraft into a sustained stall from FL350 to ocean impact in three and a half minutes. The BEA's 2012 final report singled out the "startle effect," the degradation of crew resource management as the aircraft state diverged from the crew's mental model, and the absence of meaningful training in high-altitude manual handling and alternate-law stall recovery. AF447 forced industry to acknowledge that the *automation paradox* — Lisanne Bainbridge's 1983 "Ironies of Automation" — had finally come home.

**2011 — Drift into Failure and Safety-II.** Sidney Dekker's *Drift into Failure* (2011) generalized Vaughan's normalization mechanism: organizations under resource and competitive pressure accumulate small adaptations that locally make sense, until the cumulative drift puts the system outside its design assumptions. Erik Hollnagel's parallel work on Safety-II reframed safety as the presence of successful adaptation, not merely the absence of failure — "what goes right, not just what goes wrong."

**2015 — Germanwings 9525.** On 24 March 2015 the first officer of an Airbus A320, having concealed an evolving psychotic depressive episode from his employer, locked the captain out of the cockpit during a lavatory break and flew the aircraft into the French Alps, killing 150. The BEA final report (2016) put pilot mental health, the patient-confidentiality / public-safety interface, and pilot peer support programs onto the industry agenda for the first time at scale.

**2020s — AI, RPA, FRMS, generative training tools.** Single-pilot operations research (DARPA's ALIAS / Sikorsky MATRIX program), the steady maturation of Fatigue Risk Management Systems (ICAO Doc 9966), the embedding of resilience engineering into operator manuals, and generative AI for CRM scenario design and debrief support now dominate the forward agenda.

**Synthesis.** The trajectory is unmistakable: from the individual pilot, to the cockpit team, to the organization, to the system. Each shift added a layer; none removed one. A modern accident board still asks what the pilot did (Tier 1 Acts), but it asks that question with full awareness that the answer is the beginning, not the end. The field is heading toward a synthesis in which Reason's latent failures, Vaughan's normalized deviance, Helmreich's TEM countermeasures, and Hollnagel's resilient adaptations are all routinely overlaid on the same event — including the events that go right and never become accidents.

## 3. Six Theoretical Frameworks — Each With an Anchor Accident

### 3.1 Reason's Swiss Cheese Model — Anchor: Tenerife 1977

**Origin.** James Reason, *Human Error* (Cambridge University Press, 1990) and *Managing the Risks of Organizational Accidents* (Ashgate, 1997).

**Core constructs.** Accidents result from the simultaneous alignment of holes in successive defensive layers. *Active failures* are the unsafe acts at the sharp end — the pilot's mis-set autopilot, the controller's missed callout. *Latent failures* are organizational pathogens — design decisions, procurement choices, training shortfalls, regulatory gaps — that lie dormant for months or years and require a triggering event to manifest. The defensive layers (procedures, training, hardware safeguards, supervision, regulation) each have holes; on any given day the holes are not aligned, and the system tolerates the active failures. On the day the holes line up, the accident happens.

**Tenerife as the canonical demonstration.** At Los Rodeos on 27 March 1977 the holes aligned across at least seven layers: (i) the Las Palmas bomb diversion that overcrowded a regional field; (ii) the meteorological collapse of visibility from VFR to runway-end-blind fog inside an hour; (iii) the absence of ground radar; (iv) non-standard phraseology in three places — the KLM first officer's "we are now at takeoff," the controller's "OK," and the Pan Am crew's "OK, we'll report when we're clear"; (v) a heterodyne squeal as Pan Am and the controller transmitted simultaneously, blanking the words "still on the runway" in the KLM headsets; (vi) Pan Am's missed turnoff (the crew was looking for the third taxiway exit and overshot toward the fourth); and (vii) the steep authority gradient on the KLM flight deck, where the flight engineer's question "is he not clear, that Pan American?" was overridden by Captain Veldhuyzen van Zanten's emphatic "oh, yes." Each hole alone was survivable; aligned, they killed 583 people.

**Strengths.** Universally intelligible, maps cleanly onto investigative timelines, foregrounds the organization without erasing the individual.

**Limitations.** Risks becoming a static cartoon; can encourage reverse-causal reasoning (everything in the chain was a "hole"); does not by itself tell you which latent failures matter most or how to prioritize defenses.

### 3.2 HFACS — Anchor: Czar 52 (full mapping in §4)

**Origin.** Wiegmann and Shappell, *A Human Error Approach to Aviation Accident Analysis: The Human Factors Analysis and Classification System* (Ashgate, 2003); DOT/FAA/AM-00/7 (2000). The Department of Defense adopted HFACS service-wide and has issued multiple revisions; **DoD HFACS 7.0** is the version codified in current Air Force Safety Automated System (AFSAS) practice for Class A, B, and Class E Physiological mishaps under AFI/DAFI 91-204.

**Four tiers, each with categories, sub-categories, and nanocodes.**

- **Tier 1 — Acts.** Errors (skill-based, decision, perceptual) and violations (routine, exceptional). Example nanocodes include AE101 (Unintended Operation of Equipment), AE102 (Checklist Not Followed Correctly), AE103 (Procedure Not Followed Correctly), AE104 (Over/Undercontrolled Aircraft).
- **Tier 2 — Preconditions for Unsafe Acts.** Environmental factors (physical and technological), condition of the operator (cognitive, psycho-behavioral, adverse physiological), and personnel factors (CRM/coordination, fitness for duty/self-imposed stressors).
- **Tier 3 — Supervisory Failures.** Inadequate supervision, planned inappropriate operations, failure to correct a known problem, supervisory violations.
- **Tier 4 — Organizational Influences.** Resource management, organizational climate, organizational process.

**Strengths.** Operationally tractable, mandated by service safety regulations, generates trendable data across mishaps, and explicitly forces investigators above the sharp end.

**Limitations.** Can become a check-the-box taxonomy that loses the narrative thread; nanocode proliferation creates illusory precision; less suited to near-miss and Safety-II analysis.

### 3.3 SHELL Model — Anchor: Eastern 401

**Origin.** Edwards (1972); developed by Frank Hawkins, *Human Factors in Flight* (Gower/Ashgate, 1987).

**Core constructs.** Liveware (the human operator) at the center, interacting with **S**oftware (procedures, checklists, symbology, manuals), **H**ardware (aircraft, controls, displays), **E**nvironment (physical, organizational, regulatory), and other **L**iveware (crew, ATC, maintenance). The mismatched interfaces — not the components themselves — are where accidents originate.

**Eastern 401 as the demonstration.** The L–H interface failed at the autopilot: the L-1011's CWS reversion logic was triggered by a 15-pound force on the yoke, but Eastern Airlines policy prohibited the use of CWS mode, and crews were not familiar with its behavior — a perfect L–Software (training/procedure) and L–Hardware (control logic) misalignment. The altitude alert chime was located beneath the flight engineer's workstation, but the engineer (Repo) had been dispatched to the avionics bay to check the gear visually through a small porthole — an L–E (cockpit physical environment) mismatch. The captain had an undetected brain tumor in a region affecting vision (NTSB AAR-73-14 noted but did not attribute causation) — a latent L factor. All three flight deck crew and a jumpseat technical officer were absorbed in a faulty light bulb. The aircraft descended into the Everglades at night with unrestricted visibility from a clear sky.

**Strengths.** Excellent diagnostic tool for cockpit and maintenance-deck design problems; explicitly forces examination of interfaces, not just components.

**Limitations.** Largely a static description of relationships; weaker on temporal dynamics and on organizational/latent factors above the cockpit.

### 3.4 Threat and Error Management (TEM) — Anchor: United 232 (full discussion in §5); secondary: QF32

**Origin.** Helmreich, Klinect, and Wilhelm at the University of Texas Human Factors Research Project, formalized in *System Safety and Threat and Error Management: The Line Operations Safety Audit (LOSA)* (ISAP, 2001) and adopted by ICAO Doc 9803 and successor TEM material.

**Core constructs.** **Threats** are events or errors outside the flight crew's influence that increase operational complexity (weather, terrain, ATC issues, mechanical anomalies, passenger problems, aircraft state). **Errors** are crew actions or inactions that lead to a deviation from intentions or expectations. **Undesired Aircraft States (UAS)** are flight-crew-induced aircraft conditions that clearly reduce safety margins (altitude/heading/speed deviations, incorrect automation configuration, unstable approach). The crew applies *countermeasures* — planning (briefings, contingency management), execution (monitoring/cross-check, workload management), and review (evaluation/inquiry) — to manage threats before they become errors, errors before they become UAS, and UAS before they become incidents or accidents. Per the LOSA Collaborative Archive (25+ LOSAs, 4,500+ flights), reported in the SKYbrary/ICAO publication *Defensive Flying for Pilots: An Introduction to Threat and Error Management*, "approximately 45% of the observed errors in the LOSA Archive were errors that went undetected or were not responded to by the flight crew" — making error *detection*, not error *avoidance*, the heart of TEM.

**UA232 as the canonical demonstration** (developed in §5).

**Secondary anchor — Qantas Flight 32, Singapore, 4 November 2010.** Four minutes after departure from Changi, the No. 2 Rolls-Royce Trent 900 on a Qantas A380 (VH-OQA) suffered an uncontained intermediate-pressure turbine disc failure caused by a fatigue crack in an incorrectly manufactured oil feed stub pipe. Disc fragments penetrated the wing, ruptured fuel tanks, severed wiring to engine 1, degraded engine 4, disabled one hydraulic system and the anti-lock brakes, and damaged flight controls. The five-pilot flight deck — Captain Richard de Crespigny, FO Matt Hicks, SO Mark Johnson, plus a check captain undergoing line check and the supervising check captain — worked through over 100 ECAM messages, holding for nearly two hours to stabilize aircraft state, then landed back at Changi without injury among 440 passengers and 29 crew. De Crespigny has publicly framed it explicitly in TEM terms — anticipated threats (engine problems on Trent 900s were a known fleet issue), unanticipated threats (the cascade of damage), error trajectory minimized by disciplined ECAM management and explicit role assignment.

**Strengths.** Operationally usable in real time; integrates naturally with briefings, monitoring, and debriefs; produces LOSA data that can guide training investment.

**Limitations.** Threat/error taxonomies can become bureaucratic; less suited to the rare catastrophic novel event (UA232 itself was outside the threat-anticipation envelope by design).

### 3.5 Dirty Dozen — Anchor: Aloha Airlines 243

**Origin.** Gordon Dupont, Transport Canada, 1993. Twelve maintenance-origin human factor precursors: Lack of Communication, Complacency, Lack of Knowledge, Distraction, Lack of Teamwork, Fatigue, Lack of Resources, Pressure, Lack of Assertiveness, Stress, Lack of Awareness, Norms.

**Aloha 243 as the demonstration.** On 28 April 1988 a Boeing 737-297 (N73711, "Queen Liliuokalani"), in the air on an inter-island flight from Hilo to Honolulu, lost an 18-foot section of upper fuselage in flight at FL240. Flight attendant Clarabelle Lansing was swept overboard; Captain Robert Schornstheimer (8,500 hours, 6,700 in 737) and First Officer Mimi Tompkins (8,000 hours, 3,500 in 737), working with no working interphone, no normal communication with the cabin, and 65 injured passengers behind them, landed at Kahului on Maui. NTSB AAR-89/03 (1989) found the probable cause was "the failure of the Aloha Airlines maintenance program to detect the presence of significant disbonding and fatigue damage" in the S-10L lap joint. The Dirty Dozen elements were dense: *fatigue* (inspectors working night shifts with poor lighting on aircraft that had logged 89,680 cycles, second-most in the world fleet), *complacency* and *lack of awareness* (a known service bulletin and Boeing's Aging Fleet Program had flagged lap-joint cracking; one passenger reportedly noticed a crack while boarding but did not report it), *norms* (D-checks broken into multiple shorter sessions rather than performed as a single uninterrupted inspection), and *pressure* (utilization-driven scheduling that ran the airframe at twice the cycle rate Boeing had modeled). The accident triggered the Aviation Safety Research Act of 1988 and the FAA's Aging Fleet Program.

**Strengths.** Memorable, operationally accessible to maintenance and flight crews, easy to weave into briefings.

**Limitations.** Originally maintenance-focused; lacks the systemic depth of HFACS; treated as a list rather than as the start of analysis.

### 3.6 CRM Generations 1–6 — Trigger anchor: United 173; Maturation anchor: UA232; Gen 5 anchor: US Airways 1549; Gen 6 anchor: Air France 447

**Origin.** Helmreich, Merritt, and Wilhelm, "The Evolution of Crew Resource Management Training in Commercial Aviation," *International Journal of Aviation Psychology* (1999); subsequent updates by Helmreich and colleagues, and by Kanki, Helmreich, and Anca, *Crew Resource Management* (2nd ed., 2010; 3rd ed., 2019).

- **Gen 1 — Cockpit Resource Management (1981–early 1980s).** United Airlines' Blake and Mouton "Managerial Grid"-based seminars. Psychological in tone, focus on individual leadership style. Trigger accident: **United 173 Portland 1978.**
- **Gen 2 — Crew Resource Management (mid-1980s).** Aviation-specific modules: team building, briefing strategies, situational awareness, stress management, breaking the error chain. Anchor in literature: response to Air Florida 90 (1982) and ongoing CRM-relevant accidents.
- **Gen 3 — Integration with technical training (early 1990s).** CRM moved out of the seminar room and into LOFT (Line-Oriented Flight Training) and Advanced Qualification Program (AQP) syllabi. Maturation anchor: **UA232 1989** — used industry-wide to demonstrate what trained CRM behaviors can do in extremis.
- **Gen 4 — Procedural CRM (mid-1990s).** Specific behavioral markers integrated into checklists and SOPs; CRM became evaluable.
- **Gen 5 — Error Management / TEM-integrated CRM (late 1990s–2000s).** Helmreich's recognition that error is endemic and the goal is detect, trap, mitigate rather than prevent. Anchor: **US Airways 1549 (Hudson, 15 January 2009).**
- **Gen 6 — Resilience and automation-era CRM (2010s–present).** Recognizes automation surprise, startle, mental health, and Safety-II resilience as core CRM domains. Anchor: **Air France 447 (2009)** — the accident that forced the CRM curriculum to address sustained manual flying skill, automation mode awareness, and startle/surprise training as first-order content.

**Strengths.** Frames CRM as an evolving discipline rather than a fixed curriculum; explicitly ties training generations to accident lessons.

**Limitations.** Generational boundaries are conceptual rather than crisp; "decay" of CRM attitudes between recurrent cycles is well documented (Helmreich & Taggart, 1995) and remains an open implementation challenge.

### Comparative Table — Six Frameworks

| Framework | Unit of Analysis | Error vs Threat Orientation | Operational Tractability | Military Fit |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Swiss Cheese | Defensive layers across the organization | Error (active + latent) | High (narrative) | Excellent — explains command-chain factors |
| HFACS | Acts → Preconditions → Supervision → Org Influences | Error (taxonomic) | High (codified) | Native — DoD HFACS 7.0 is mandated |
| SHELL | Liveware-centered interfaces | Error (interface mismatch) | Moderate (diagnostic) | Good for cockpit/maintenance design |
| TEM | Threat → Error → UAS, countermeasures | Threat-led | Very high (real-time) | Excellent — anticipates mission threats |
| Dirty Dozen | Twelve precursor states | Error (precursor) | High (mnemonic) | Good for maintenance, useful in briefings |
| CRM Gens 1–6 | Crew behavior over time | Both (evolving) | High (training-coupled) | Excellent — direct training input |

The frameworks are *complementary lenses*, not competing theories. A mature investigation overlays all six on the same event.

## 4. Case Study A — Czar 52 (B-52H, Fairchild AFB, 24 June 1994)

**Authoritative sources.** USAF Aircraft Accident Investigation Board report convened under AFR 110-14 (September 1994); Tony Kern, *Darker Shades of Blue: A Case Study of Failed Leadership* (1995); subsequent academic case studies (Albany Scholars Archive; California Training Institute).

### 4.1 Sequence of Events

At 13:58 PDT on 24 June 1994, B-52H tail number 61-0026, callsign **Czar 52**, departed Fairchild AFB for a practice profile for the upcoming Sunday airshow. The crew: Lt Col Arthur "Bud" Holland (aircraft commander, 92nd Bomb Wing Chief of Standardization and Evaluation; 46 years old, 5,275.3 total hours, 5,038.3 hours in B-52); Lt Col Mark McGeehan (copilot, commander of the 325th Bomb Squadron, 38); Lt Col Kenneth Huston (radar navigator, 325th Operations Officer, 41); Col Robert Wolff (safety observer, 92nd Bomb Wing Vice Commander, 46), flying his "fini flight." The mission profile called for low-altitude passes, 60° banked turns, a steep climb, and a touch-and-go on Runway 23.

Most of the profile was completed without incident. Approaching the touch-and-go, a KC-135 occupied Runway 23, so the tower instructed Czar 52 to go around. Maintaining about 250 ft AGL — the B-52's wingspan is 185 ft — Holland requested and was granted a 360° left turn around the tower. To avoid restricted airspace behind the tower (associated with a nuclear weapons storage facility), Holland tightened the turn. At 182 KIAS entering the turn, with engines requiring up to eight seconds to spool, airspeed bled off to 145 KIAS as the bank passed 60°, then 90°. Eight seconds before impact, full right spoiler, right rudder, and aft elevator were applied. At 14:16 PDT the aircraft rolled past 90° of bank, departed controlled flight, and struck the ground near the weapons storage area in approximately a 95° angle of bank. All four crew members were killed. McGeehan was in an ejection seat and partially ejected at impact; Huston did not initiate ejection; Wolff's observer seat was not ejection-capable.

### 4.2 The Bud Holland File

The accident did not begin on 24 June 1994. The AIB documented a multi-year pattern that the chain of command knew about and tolerated:

- **1991, softball game.** Holland circled a B-52 at 65° of bank over his daughter's softball game, beginning at 2,500 ft AGL; bank reportedly increased to 80°; the aircraft lost 1,000 ft in what one witness described as a "death spiral."
- **19 May 1991, Fairchild airshow.** Holland was the airshow command pilot. The demonstration violated bank, pitch, and altitude limits, flew directly over spectators, and possibly violated altitude restrictions. The wing staff observed; no action.
- **12 July 1991, change-of-command flyover.** Holland flew the B-52 below 100 ft AGL with steep turns >45° and a wingover. Wing Commander Col Arne Weinman and DO Col Arnold Julich verbally reprimanded Holland. No formal action.
- **17 May 1992, Fairchild airshow.** Repeat performance — low-altitude steep turns >45°, high pitch climb, wingover.
- **1993–1994.** A 1993 sortie with another aircrew, during which Holland exceeded bank limits aggressively, led the crew to refuse to fly with him again and to brief squadron leadership. Lt Col McGeehan, commanding the 325th Bomb Squadron, attempted to ground Holland. He was overruled. McGeehan thereafter committed to flying as Holland's copilot personally to protect his aircrews.

The pattern was visible. It was reported. Across multiple commanders, multiple opportunities, the system declined to act.

### 4.3 HFACS Mapping

A clean DoD HFACS 7.0 mapping of Czar 52 — emphasizing how the weight is loaded above the cockpit:

- **Tier 1 — Acts.** Holland: violation, exceptional (intentional disregard of bank, pitch, and altitude limits); skill-based error (allowed airspeed to bleed below sustainable in the turn). McGeehan: did not or could not override the command pilot in the seconds available.
- **Tier 2 — Preconditions.** Mental state of overconfidence; established cockpit gradient; crew composition (Wolff added to the schedule the morning of the flight as a fini-flight observer, without full mission planning involvement).
- **Tier 3 — Supervisory.** This is the heavy-loaded tier. *Failed to correct a known problem* — multiple commanders had documented Holland's pattern across three years and had failed to take effective corrective action. *Supervisory violations* — approval of demonstration maneuvers that fell outside what the B-52 community accepted as safe practice. *Planned inappropriate operations* — the AIB found the practice profile itself contained maneuvers beyond aircraft limits.
- **Tier 4 — Organizational Influences.** *Organizational climate* — a culture at the 92nd Bomb Wing that accepted Holland's "hot stick" reputation as an asset rather than a hazard. *Organizational process* — the airshow demonstration program lacked rigorous external review and standardization. Col Pellerin, Assistant DO at the time of the mishap, was eventually court-martialed on three counts of dereliction of duty (failure to obtain proper approval for the airshow maneuvers, failure to ensure a safe routine, failure to ground Holland after repeated violations).

### 4.4 Reason Overlay

Latent failures — wing-level tolerance of standards violations, an airshow approval process without effective external oversight, a culture that rewarded "hot sticks," a supervisory chain that prioritized squadron morale over enforcement — aligned with active failures (the bank, the airspeed, the proximity to ground) at one moment in 1994. The active failures were the spark; the latent failures were the powder.

### 4.5 Normalization of Deviance in Action

Each accepted boundary push reset the standard. After the 1991 softball-game incident with no consequence, the next year's airshow could go further. After the 1992 airshow with only verbal reprimand, the 1993–1994 sorties pushed further still. Vaughan's mechanism is visible in transcripts: junior officers describing Holland's flying as "amazing"; senior officers describing the pattern as "his style"; everyone present believing that he had always recovered and would always recover, until he didn't.

### 4.6 Lessons for a Trainer Squadron

- **Authority gradient cuts both ways.** McGeehan tried to use his position as squadron commander to ground a command pilot at his own wing and was overruled. The lesson is not that the gradient was too steep — it was that the *upward* path was insufficient.
- **Supervisory courage is the failure mode.** Every step in the Holland file had at least one person who knew and could have acted. The cost of action was reputational and political; the cost of inaction was four crew, an airframe, and a permanent stain on a wing's history.
- **Reducing the accident to "rogue pilot" erases the lesson.** The AIB explicitly did not stop at Holland. Neither should you, when the topic comes up in your squadron.
- **The trainer-squadron analog.** Every IP pool contains a small number of pilots whose flying others quietly avoid. The squadron that names that problem and addresses it institutionally — through stan-eval flags, peer feedback, command involvement — prevents its own Czar 52. The squadron that doesn't, doesn't.

## 5. Case Study B — United Airlines 232 (DC-10, Sioux Gateway, 19 July 1989)

**Authoritative sources.** NTSB Aircraft Accident Report AAR-90/06 (1990); Captain Al Haynes' public lectures (Air Safety Foundation, NASA Ames, NTSB Training Center, university talks, 1991–2019); conversation analysis of the CVR by Lehmann and colleagues (MDPI, 2025).

### 5.1 Sequence of Events

United 232, a McDonnell Douglas DC-10-10 (N1819U) en route from Stapleton (Denver) to O'Hare (Chicago) with 285 passengers and 11 crew, departed at 14:09 CDT on 19 July 1989. At 15:16 CDT, while in cruise at FL370 over northwest Iowa, the No. 2 (tail-mounted) engine's stage-1 fan disk suffered a fatigue-crack-initiated separation, an uncontained failure. Disk fragments severed the lines of all three hydraulic systems where they passed through a single ten-inch route in the empennage. The DC-10's three independent hydraulic systems had been designed against the precise failure that occurred.

With zero hydraulic pressure to any control surface, the airplane assumed a slight right-turn tendency from the failure-state position of the ailerons, and entered a phugoid oscillation losing roughly 1,500 ft per cycle. Captain Alfred C. Haynes (57, 29,967 total hours), First Officer William Records, and Flight Engineer Dudley Dvorak determined that the only available means of control was differential thrust on the two wing-mounted engines. Senior flight attendant Jan Brown Lohr identified a deadheading United training check airman in the cabin — Captain Dennis E. "Denny" Fitch, 46, with substantial DC-10 instructional experience and prior simulator work on hydraulics-loss scenarios after Japan Air Lines 123. Per the CVR (Mid-America Air Museum 35th Anniversary presentation, Sioux City, July 2024), Fitch entered the cockpit at 15:29:35 CDT and "sees the pilots struggling on the yoke." Fitch knelt between the two pilot seats and worked the throttles with both hands while Haynes flew, Records backed up the throttle inputs, and Dvorak worked the systems and the radios with United dispatch and SFO maintenance.

Forty-four minutes after the failure, at 16:00 CDT, UA232 landed on closed Runway 22 at Sioux Gateway Airport. Touchdown was approximately 240 knots and 1,850 ft/min descent — a target landing would have been 140 knots and 300 ft/min — with no flaps or slats, the right wing low. The aircraft cartwheeled. Of 296 on board, 184 survived. All four flight deck crew survived with serious injuries; Fitch suffered multiple fractures and organ injuries and nearly died. NTSB AAR-90/06 found the crew's performance was "highly commendable and greatly exceeded reasonable expectations" — expert United and McDonnell Douglas pilots in post-accident simulator reconstructions could not reproduce a survivable landing.

### 5.2 TEM Mapping

- **Threat (catastrophic, novel, unanticipated).** Total loss of all hydraulic flight-control authority — a failure mode the airframe was certified against by design.
- **Errors trajectory.** Minimal. The crew did not correctly *fly* the airplane in any normal sense; they invented a new control regime in real time. Errors were small, transient, and continuously detected. Phugoid oscillations were managed rather than eliminated.
- **Undesired Aircraft State.** Continuous — the entire 44-minute flight was a UAS by any normal definition.
- **Countermeasures.** Planning (rapid contingency development with Fitch on differential thrust; consultation with company maintenance via radio); execution (explicit role assignment, cross-monitoring); review (rolling reassessment as the situation deteriorated).

### 5.3 CRM Behaviors Actually Demonstrated

This is the part of UA232 that transfers directly to a T-6A IP/SP cockpit. The behaviors are nameable:

- **Workload distribution under impossible load.** Haynes assigned roles explicitly: Fitch on throttles, Records on backup throttles and primary flight monitoring, Dvorak on systems and external communications, Haynes on the radios with ATC and overall command. Nobody was doing everybody's job.
- **Distributed situational awareness.** Each crew member maintained a different mental model of the aircraft state and the situation; they cross-talked frequently in plain language to keep the models consolidated.
- **Inquiry and advocacy.** The crew openly questioned assumptions ("you want them now?" — Records on the flap extension attempt), tested hypotheses, and discarded approaches that didn't work without ego cost.
- **Leadership and followership.** Haynes accepted Fitch into the cockpit and onto the controls without hesitation when Fitch's expertise exceeded what the seated crew could offer — a textbook expansion of the team boundary. Fitch followed Haynes' command authority while exercising independent technical judgment on the throttles.
- **Plain-language communication.** Conversation analysis of the CVR shows the crew dropped most jargon and reverted to plain English with ATC ("we have no controls" rather than a technical hydraulics description), an appropriate context-driven adaptation.

### 5.4 Lessons

- **Reducing UA232 to "miracle" erases the trainable behaviors.** Haynes himself, in his public lectures from 1991 until his death in 2019, listed five factors that contributed to the outcome: luck, communications, preparation, execution, and cooperation. He did not lead with luck.
- **The value of distributed expertise.** Fitch was a passenger. The squadron implication is direct: the IP in the back seat, the SOF, the duty officer, the experienced wingman — these are crew members on your sortie even if they are not in your cockpit.
- **The limits of standardization.** No checklist covered total hydraulic loss in the DC-10. The crew built a procedure in 44 minutes. Standardization is necessary, but the *adaptive* capacity that comes from CRM training and from accepting input outside the normal authority chain is what produced the outcome.
- **CRM as a behavioral repertoire, not a vocabulary.** "Communicate" is a platitude. "Assign roles explicitly under workload" is a behavior.

## 6. Comparative Cases

### 6.1 C-17A 00-0173, Joint Base Elmendorf-Richardson, 28 July 2010

A 3rd Wing / 176th Wing C-17A (tail 00-0173, *Spirit of the Aleutians*) on a training sortie for the Arctic Thunder airshow stalled at high bank angle and crashed 58 seconds after takeoff. All four crew were killed. The mishap pilot, Maj Michael Freyholtz, had developed a steep-turn aerial demonstration profile that he had flown repeatedly in 2009 demonstrations. The AIB report (released December 2010) found that during a 260° reversal turn five seconds after the stall warning activated, bank increased to 62°, then to 82° as he applied full right rudder and aft stick; descent rate reached 9,000 ft/min. Pilot error, low-altitude stall, intentional violation of demonstrated maneuver limits — the parallels to Czar 52 are explicit and were noted publicly by aviation analysts at the time (John Nance, ABC News, on the FlightGlobal coverage). Same pattern, 16 years later: a respected pilot, supervisory tolerance of an unsafe profile, normalization of boundary violations. **Dominant framework lens.** HFACS Tier 3/4 and Vaughan's normalization mechanism. **Transferable lesson.** The institutional memory of Czar 52 was not enough to prevent its near-perfect replay — meaning continuous active enforcement, not historical awareness alone, is what matters.

### 6.2 Polish Air Force Tu-154M, Smolensk, 10 April 2010

The Polish Air Force 36th Regiment Tu-154M PLF 101, carrying President Lech Kaczyński and 95 others to a Katyń commemoration, attempted a non-precision approach into Smolensk Severny — a former military aerodrome — in dense fog with visibility around 400 m. The crew descended below decision altitude despite a TAWS "TERRAIN AHEAD" warning, struck trees, and crashed short and left of the runway. All 96 on board were killed. The MAK (Russian) and KBWLLP (Polish) commissions both found pilot error as the proximate cause, though their conclusions diverged sharply on the role of Russian air-traffic-control information and Polish accusations of evidence handling — a controversy that remains politically charged. The investigators agreed that the cockpit environment included the Commander-in-Chief of the Polish Air Force present in the cockpit during the final approach, with a navigator captured on CVR saying "he'll go crazy" — a possible reference to a feared reaction from the President had they diverted. **Dominant framework lens.** Authority gradient, mission-completion bias, "important passenger" effect. **Transferable lesson.** When the people in your back seat or on your manifest have more political weight than your decision authority, your real go/no-go authority has already been compromised. Crew briefings for any high-profile mission should explicitly empty the back-seat passenger of any operational say.

### 6.3 US Airways 1549, Hudson River, 15 January 2009

Roughly three and a half minutes after departure from LaGuardia, US Airways 1549 (Airbus A320-214, N106US, Captain Chesley Sullenberger, FO Jeffrey Skiles) struck a flock of Canada geese at 2,818 ft AGL and lost almost all thrust in both CFM56-5B engines. Twelve seconds after impact, Sullenberger took control ("my aircraft") and directed Skiles ("get the QRH loss of thrust on both engines") — appropriate given Sullenberger's 4,765 hours in the A320 versus Skiles' 37 hours fresh out of type training. Sullenberger ditched in the Hudson River; all 155 occupants survived. NTSB AAR-10/03 (May 2010) found the probable cause to be bird ingestion and credited as contributing to survivability: "the decision-making of the flight crewmembers and their crew resource management during the accident sequence." Same TEM framework as UA232, very different threat structure — a brief, intense window rather than a prolonged emergency; a well-trained type-specific automation envelope rather than a total system loss. **Dominant framework lens.** TEM Gen-5 CRM with clean role assignment and workload management under extreme time compression. **Transferable lesson.** Speed of role allocation matters more than depth of communication when the clock is short. "My aircraft" and "get the QRH" inside the first 20 seconds.

### 6.4 Air France 447, Atlantic, 1 June 2009

A330-203 F-GZCP, Rio–Paris, lost airspeed indications in cruise when pitot tubes iced over while transiting the Intertropical Convergence Zone. The autopilot disconnected, the flight control system reverted to alternate law, and the pilot flying (Bonin, 32, in the right seat) made sustained nose-up sidestick inputs that took the aircraft to 38,000 ft and into a sustained stall, descending in a stalled state at up to 20,000 ft/min for nearly three and a half minutes to ocean impact. All 228 on board were killed. The BEA final report (July 2012) cited the startle effect at autopilot disconnect, the degradation of CRM as the situation diverged from the crew's mental model (75 stall warnings sounded; the crew never identified the stall), the absence of an angle-of-attack display, and the inadequacy of training in high-altitude manual flying and alternate-law handling. **Dominant framework lens.** Bainbridge's automation paradox (1983); Reason's latent failures in training program design; CRM Gen 6. **Transferable lesson.** Sustained automation reliance creates a manual-skill deficit that no checklist can paper over when the automation hands control back unexpectedly. T-6A glass cockpit / HUD use has the same dynamic in miniature — when the magenta line fails, you fly the airplane with the basic instruments and your hands.

### 6.5 Asiana 214, San Francisco International, 6 July 2013

A Boeing 777-200ER on a visual approach to Runway 28L at SFO with the ILS glideslope inoperative for the day. The captain (Lee Kang Kuk) was undergoing initial operating experience on the 777 with an instructor pilot (Lee Jung Min) seated in the right. The approach was high; the captain selected FLCH (Flight Level Change) mode on the autopilot, which inadvertently left the autothrottle in an idle state (HOLD) where it did not maintain commanded speed. Airspeed decayed below VREF. None of the three pilots monitored the airspeed adequately or initiated a go-around when the approach became unstable. The 777 struck the seawall short of the runway; three of the 307 occupants were killed (one of the three was run over by a rescue vehicle on the runway). NTSB AAR-14/01 (June 2014) found the probable cause was the crew's mismanagement of the approach combined with over-reliance on automation that they "did not fully understand," and identified the cultural authority gradient — the captain's reluctance to call a go-around because he felt only the instructor pilot held that authority — as contributing. **Dominant framework lens.** Automation mode confusion (Sarter & Woods) + cultural authority gradient (Helmreich & Merritt). **Transferable lesson.** "Unable to comply" is a complete sentence. So is "going around." Both must be sayable across every rank and culture gradient in your squadron, including from SP to IP.

### 6.6 Honest Gap Statement

Open-source Hellenic Air Force mishap reports are not publicly available in the form and depth used elsewhere in this document. Comparable European trainer-environment patterns are drawn from RAF Military Aircraft Accident Summaries, Luftwaffe Bundeswehr investigations, and Aeronautica Militare Italiana (AMI) releasable material where it has been published. The patterns — IP/SP gradient effects, demonstration-flight tolerance, fatigue on back-to-back training sorties — are consistent across air forces, even where the specific reports cannot be cited.

## 7. Cross-Cutting Deep Dives

### 7.1 Normalization of Deviance and Drift into Failure

Diane Vaughan, *The Challenger Launch Decision* (University of Chicago Press, 1996), defined the mechanism in one sentence: *"Social normalization of deviance means that people within the organization become so much accustomed to a deviation that they don't consider it as deviant, despite the fact that they far exceed their own rules for the elementary safety."* Sidney Dekker, *Drift into Failure* (2011), generalized it as the cumulative, locally rational adaptations that take an organization outside its safe envelope without any single person deciding to do so.

**Czar 52** — boundary erosion over multiple airshow seasons. Each tolerated violation was a new datum that "we can do this." The 1994 turn was inside the envelope Holland had established over the preceding three years. The envelope was outside what the airplane could do.

**C-17 Elmendorf 2010** — same pattern. Maj Freyholtz had flown the demonstration profile repeatedly in 2009. Each prior performance reinforced its acceptability. The accident profile differed only marginally from earlier ones.

**Challenger 1986** — O-ring erosion observed on launches before STS-51-L was treated as "in family" — within the range of previously observed behavior. The Rogers Commission and subsequent Vaughan analysis demonstrated that the engineering community had reset its baseline to include damage that had originally been a Criticality-1 design violation.

**Columbia 2003** — foam shedding from the external tank had been observed for years and was classified as a maintenance issue rather than a safety-of-flight issue, despite the original design intent of zero foam loss. STS-107 was lost when a foam strike on the leading edge of the left wing during launch breached the thermal protection system, leading to wing failure during reentry.

**Recognition signals — squadron-applicable.**
- *"We've always done it this way."* A statement that an action is normal, made without any reference to whether it is permitted.
- *"No one got hurt last time."* A statement that the operationally-relevant data is the absence of bad outcomes from past instances, rather than the design margin.
- *"He's our best pilot."* A statement that the rule does not apply to this individual.
- *Local heroes who get away with it.* Pilots whose tolerated violations become squadron lore.

If you hear any of these on the flight line, you are listening to the early phase of an accident sequence.

### 7.2 Fatigue Science

Fatigue is not tiredness; it is a physiological state with measurable cognitive consequences. Treating it as a willpower problem is the equivalent of treating hypoxia as a willpower problem.

**Two-process model (Borbély, 1982).** Sleep/wake regulation is governed by two interacting processes: the circadian process (Process C), driven by the suprachiasmatic nucleus and the body's roughly 24-hour clock, and the sleep homeostat (Process S), which builds wake-related sleep pressure during continuous wakefulness and dissipates it during sleep. Performance is degraded when Process S is high (long wake time / sleep debt) and Process C is low (the window of circadian low, WOCL, roughly 03:00–05:00 for a day-adapted human).

**Sleep debt.** Cumulative shortfall from individual baseline sleep requirement (typically 7–9 hours per 24). Five consecutive nights of 4–5 hours produce cognitive impairment comparable to 24 hours of continuous wakefulness.

**Microsleeps.** Involuntary 1–15 second sleep episodes that occur during sustained wakefulness, particularly within the WOCL. The pilot is not aware they have occurred. They are sufficient to miss a callout, an altitude alert, or a TCAS RA.

**Cognitive impairment equivalents (Dawson & Reid, *Nature* 388:235, 1997; Lamond & Dawson, *J Sleep Res* 1999; Williamson & Feyer, 2000).** 17–19 hours of continuous wakefulness produces psychomotor performance equivalent to a 0.05% blood alcohol concentration. 24 hours equals approximately 0.10% BAC. The takeaway for an IP: a 04:00 alert with a full briefing/preflight/sortie/debrief day puts you in the 17-hour zone before the sun sets.

**Fatigue Risk Management Systems (FRMS).** Codified in ICAO Doc 9966 (*Manual for the Oversight of Fatigue Management Approaches*). Combines (a) hard duty/rest limits, (b) bio-mathematical modeling (SAFE, FAID, FRI, FAST/SAFTE), (c) individual fatigue reporting, (d) incident-data feedback loops.

**Military-specific issues.**
- *Sustained operations.* 24+ hour combat sorties, alert shifts, night ops where the WOCL collides with critical task workload.
- *Trainer-syndrome.* Back-to-back IP sorties — particularly in the SUPT environment with multiple students per day — compress duty into windows where the IP fatigues faster than the airframe.
- *Show-time and finis-flight pressure.* The same factor that put Wolff in the Czar 52 cockpit on short notice.

**Anchor accidents.**
- **Colgan 3407 (Buffalo, 12 February 2009).** Bombardier Q400 stalled on approach; captain pulled aft on stick shaker activation rather than pushing forward; 50 fatalities. NTSB AAR-10/01 found "the pilots' performance was likely impaired because of fatigue, but the extent of their impairment and the degree to which it contributed to the performance deficiencies that occurred during the flight cannot be conclusively determined." The first officer had commuted from Seattle to Newark overnight via jumpseat; the captain had been overnight at Newark accessing the company computer at 21:51, 03:00, and 07:30. NTSB Chair Hersman called explicit attention to fatigue; the political reform process produced the 1,500-hour ATP rule and Part 117 flight/duty/rest regulations.
- **American 1420 (Little Rock, 1 June 1999).** MD-82 overran the runway in a severe thunderstorm landing; 11 killed. NTSB found fatigue and continuation bias in a long-duty-day crew were contributing factors.
- **B-1B Diego Garcia 2013.** Sortie-end mishap (releasable details limited) commonly used as a military-side parallel for fatigue effects on long-duration combat operations.
- **Air India Express 812 (Mangalore, 22 May 2010).** Captain reportedly napped through most of the cruise, was disoriented on approach, and overran the runway at landing; 158 killed. Cited as a shorthaul fatigue case where in-cruise sleep replaced adequate rest.

### 7.3 Military-Specific Stressors

**Authority gradient.** Military hierarchy is steeper than civilian by design — and that is a feature in combat, a bug in CRM. The trainer environment amplifies the gradient: a student is being evaluated continuously, and the IP holds career-impacting authority. Tenerife showed what steep gradient does on a civilian flight deck; Smolensk showed what steep gradient does when the passenger is the head of state.

**Mission-completion bias.** "Press-on-itis" and "get-home-itis." Documented across both military and civilian accident sets: a crew progresses past go/no-go decision points because of mission importance, schedule pressure, or social/political weight of the passenger. Antidotes: explicit pre-mission divert criteria, named decision points, and command-level support for the divert.

**Physiological factors.**
- *Hypoxia* (especially insidious in unpressurized or rapidly depressurized cockpits — the T-6A has experienced documented physiological events).
- *G-LOC and A-LOC.* G-induced loss of consciousness and almost-loss of consciousness. The T-6A is capable of +7/-3.5 G; G-tolerance is not a binary, and is degraded by dehydration, fatigue, and recent illness.
- *Spatial disorientation.* The leading killer in fighter and trainer aviation in low-visibility or night flight. Type I (unrecognized) is most dangerous.
- *Fatigue under high op tempo.* See §7.2.

**The two-seat training cockpit as a CRM environment.** Underexamined in the literature. The IP/SP dyad is structurally analogous to a captain/first officer dyad with three differences that matter: (a) the upward inquiry path is steeper than civilian (because of evaluation authority), (b) the workload distribution is asymmetric and variable across the sortie (handing off control between IP and SP repeatedly), and (c) the SP's mental model is by definition incomplete. Squadron CRM standards must explicitly address each.

### 7.4 Just Culture vs Blame Culture

James Reason (1997) and Sidney Dekker (*Just Culture: Balancing Safety and Accountability*, 2007, 2nd ed. 2012) developed the framework now used across high-hazard industries. A *just culture* distinguishes:

- **Honest error / human error.** Inadvertent slips, lapses, or mistakes — the operator's intent was correct, the execution was wrong. Substitution test (Reason): *"Substitute the individual concerned, for someone else coming from the same domain of activity and possessing comparable qualifications and experience [and ask] would they have behaved any differently?"* If the answer is no, the focus belongs on the system, not the individual.
- **At-risk behavior.** A conscious choice in the face of risk, where the risk is not recognized or is mistakenly believed to be justified. Coaching, training, and system redesign are the appropriate responses.
- **Reckless behavior.** Conscious choice to take a substantial and unjustifiable risk — disregard for safety. Discipline is appropriate.

A *blame culture* punishes all three categories indistinguishably, suppresses reporting (the data that safety lives on), and degrades safety overall. Andrew Hopkins, *Lessons from Longford* (2000) and *Failure to Learn* (2008), drew the same lesson from the 1998 Longford gas plant explosion (Esso Australia, two killed, gas supply to Victoria cut for two weeks) and the 2005 BP Texas City refinery explosion (15 killed, 180 injured): organizations that punished operators for reporting safety issues lost the information needed to prevent the catastrophic event.

**Squadron application.** The mishap-prevention chain from a routine reportable error to the next Class A runs through the willingness to report. Build a reporting environment where honest error is met with curiosity and a fix; at-risk behavior is met with coaching; and reckless behavior is met with consequences. Conflating the categories collapses the chain.

### 7.5 Automation Surprise and Mode Confusion

Lisanne Bainbridge's "Ironies of Automation" (*Automatica* 19(6):775–779, 1983) framed the central paradox: *"when manual take-over is needed there is likely to be something wrong in the process, so that unusual actions will be needed to control it, and one can argue that the operator needs to be more rather than less skilled."* Automation deskills the operator precisely in the regime where, when the automation fails, the operator most needs the skill. Parasuraman and Riley (*Human Factors* 39(2), 1997) typed the failure modes as *use, misuse, disuse, and abuse* of automation.

**Anchor: Air France 447.** The crew did not understand the alternate-law degradation, did not maintain pitch awareness in cruise without the flight director, and made sustained nose-up inputs that the airframe in alternate law no longer protected against. The BEA's recommendations FRAN-2012-041, -045, and -046 all targeted manual-flying competence and startle/surprise training.

**Secondary: Asiana 214.** The captain selected FLCH and did not understand that the autothrottle had reverted to HOLD and would not protect speed. The mode-confusion problem (Sarter & Woods, *Human Factors* 1995) was front and center.

**T-6A implication.** The Texan II's glass cockpit, HUD, and integrated avionics suite create the same potential dynamic in miniature. The discipline is unchanged from the airliner case: knowledge of mode hierarchy, active monitoring with cross-check, and frequent manual flying to prevent skill decay. The IP/SP cockpit is the right place to enforce this — a student who only flies the magenta line learns to fly the magenta line, not the airplane.

## 8. Squadron-Level Application

### 8.1 CRM/TEM Scenarios for the T-6A Environment

Scenarios should be built around the operational threat set, not generic CRM platitudes. High-value examples:

- *Engine PCL anomaly low-altitude.* Threat: PCL/torque mismatch in the pattern. Tests: workload allocation, communication with IP, decision to declare/return.
- *Bird strike/canopy crack at altitude.* TEM threat trajectory; physiological consequences (slipstream, hearing loss, cold exposure).
- *Inadvertent IMC during low-level/contact sortie.* SD-prone scenario; tests instrument transition discipline.
- *Hypoxia event simulated.* Recognition, ROBD or simulator-based; team callout discipline.
- *Departure from controlled flight in advanced phase.* Tests recovery procedure discipline under startle.
- *IP incapacitation.* Often skipped; the SP must fly and communicate. Worth running in the simulator under controlled conditions.

### 8.2 Briefing and Debriefing Techniques

**Briefing structure.** Threat identification (TEM-based) in the mission planning phase: weather, fatigue (self and crew), currency, aircraft status, formation dynamics, low-level route hazards, airfield/airspace issues, training-specific risks. Explicit divert criteria. Named decision points. Crew brief covers role assignment and contingency authority — especially "Knock-it-off" call standards.

**Debriefing structure.** The *DESC* model (Describe — Express — Suggest — Consequences) for addressing observed performance issues. The *STEAR* model (Setup — Threats — Errors — Actions — Results) for structured TEM-based debrief. Both are far more useful than free-form "let's talk about the flight."

**Good ORM practices.** Operational Risk Management as actually used — not as a checkbox. Caution-range mission scoring (as the Elmendorf C-17 crew did, accurately, the morning of the mishap) is *valid data*; if the mission is in caution range, the response is to mitigate the threats, not to fly it as if the score were green.

### 8.3 Threat Identification in Mission Planning

A minimum threat set for every T-6A sortie:
- Weather (ceilings, visibility, winds, density altitude, turbulence/icing, convective potential).
- Crew fatigue (objective: sleep in prior 24 and 48 hours; subjective: Samn-Perelli or NASA-TLX rating).
- Currency (event currency, aircraft currency, weather currency).
- Aircraft status (open write-ups, deferred maintenance, recent component changes).
- Formation dynamics (lead/wing experience asymmetry, comm plan, lost-comm procedures).
- Low-level / area considerations (terrain, restricted airspace, MOAs, range schedules).
- Mission-specific (instrument approach minima, night, syllabus event difficulty).

### 8.4 Building and Sustaining a Healthy Safety Culture

- **How to report.** ASAP/MFOQA/USAF Safety Net or equivalent. Anonymous-where-needed pathways. Squadron safety officer as a known and trusted role.
- **How to receive a report.** The substitution test, not the disciplinary instinct. Curiosity first. Fix-the-system second. Discipline only for reckless behavior — and only after the substitution test has been honestly run.
- **Visible command support.** Squadron leadership flying with the FRMS program, attending safety meetings, calling knock-it-offs on themselves and being visible about it.

### 8.5 Recognizing and Challenging "Bud Holland" Patterns

The squadron-applicable test is simple:

- Is there a pilot who others quietly avoid flying with?
- Is there a pilot whose violations are described with admiration ("oh, that's just X")?
- Is there a pilot whose name appears repeatedly in informal hangar talk about boundary-pushing?
- Does the squadron have an unspoken short list of "events" that are tolerated but not approved?

If the answer to any of these is yes, supervisory courage is the only intervention that works. The conversation is uncomfortable. The alternative is not. McGeehan's outcome is a permanent reminder that *the individual courage of one squadron commander, unsupported by the chain above, is not enough.* Squadron-level intervention must connect upward to wing/group with documented evidence, and downward with consistent enforcement of standards every sortie.

## 9. Future Trends and Recommendations

### 9.1 Forward Trends (5–10 year horizon)

- **AI co-pilot / decision support.** DARPA's ALIAS (Aircrew Labor In-Cockpit Automation System) program, executed in part by Sikorsky/Lockheed Martin with the MATRIX autonomy stack, has flown the Optionally Piloted Black Hawk fully uncrewed. Per DARPA's official announcement and *Defense News* (8 February 2022), "Sikorsky, a Lockheed Martin company, completed 30-minutes of uninhabited flight with the optionally piloted vehicle (OPV) over the U.S. Army installation at Fort Campbell, Kentucky on February 5th," with a second uninhabited flight on 7 February 2022 at Sabre Army Heliport. Project Convergence 2022 (October 2022) demonstrated autonomous internal/external cargo resupply missions for the U.S. Army. On 14 October 2024, per a Lockheed Martin press release, "Sikorsky…has received a $6 million award from the Defense Advanced Research Projects Agency (DARPA) to install the company's ALIAS/MATRIX™ flight autonomy system onto the U.S. Army's experimental fly-by-wire UH-60M Black Hawk® helicopter. Designated MX…" with integration scheduled for 2025 and testing covering "single pilot operation to fully uninhabited flight." Garmin Autoland (certified 2020 on the Piper M600 and HondaJet) is the precedent for emergency-autonomous descent and landing in light-jet/single-pilot operations. The trajectory toward reduced-crew and single-pilot operations is real and accelerating; squadrons should be developing training models for human-AI teaming now.
- **UAS/RPA human factors.** Documented MQ-9/MQ-1 operator fatigue, distributed crew situational awareness, and the psychological load of "warfare from a trailer" (Chappelle and colleagues, AFRL, 2014 onward). The absence of vestibular and proprioceptive cues, the latency-bridged decision making, and the home-life/operations interface produce different fatigue and stress signatures than manned operations. Trainer squadrons that may feed both manned and unmanned pipelines should be aware.
- **Neuro-physiological monitoring.** HRV (heart rate variability) for workload, EEG for vigilance, eye-tracking for SA and gaze entropy. Increasingly available as cockpit-embeddable sensor packages. Ethical limits: who sees the data, who acts on it, how it interacts with medical certification.
- **FRMS maturation.** Bio-mathematical scheduling (SAFTE-FAST, FAID) integrated with rostering systems; individual variability accounted for via wearable sleep monitors; biomarker integration (cortisol, melatonin in some research settings).
- **Mental health post-Germanwings.** Peer support programs (the Pilot Assistance Network was cited as a good-practice example in the BEA's final report on Germanwings 9525), destigmatization, careful management of the regulator/medical-confidentiality interface. EASA Commission Regulation (EU) 2018/1042 mandated pilot peer support across European operators. Equivalent USAF pathways exist through Aerospace and Operational Physiology channels.
- **Safety-II / Resilience engineering (Hollnagel, Dekker).** Increasingly mainstream — asks not only what went wrong but what went right and why most days produce safe outcomes despite the same threat set. Squadron debriefs that capture *what we did right* are as valuable as those that focus on what we did wrong.
- **VR/XR and generative AI in training.** Affordable VR for CRM scenarios, AI-driven debrief assistants that analyze CVR-equivalent intercom recordings, generative scenario authoring. Useful — provided they augment rather than replace the IP-led debrief.

### 9.2 Concrete Squadron-Level Recommendations

1. **Adopt a TEM-structured mission brief and debrief template.** Standardize across the squadron: threats identified before the sortie; errors, UAS, and countermeasures discussed after. Make it short, mandatory, and visible.
2. **Run a quarterly squadron-level FRMS check.** Track aggregate sleep and duty data for the IP cadre; surface trends to commanders before fatigue becomes the contributing factor to a Class A. Use objective bio-mathematical model output, not vibes.
3. **Establish and protect a peer-support / mental-health pathway.** Identify a trained peer point of contact in the squadron; brief it at every newcomer in-processing; have command visibly use it. Treat mental health as a flight safety issue.
4. **Implement a "supervisory courage" practice.** Stan-eval and command leadership review boundary-push reports monthly. The standard is McGeehan's standard: if a pilot is identified as a pattern risk, the chain acts. Document the absence of action as well as action.
5. **Build a "Czar 52 / Elmendorf C-17 / UA232 / AF447" case-study rotation into recurrent training.** One case per quarter, IP-led, with structured discussion (the §10 questions are a starting point). Treat institutional memory as a perishable skill.
6. **Audit automation/manual-flying balance in the syllabus.** Ensure students and IPs alike hand-fly the airplane regularly — not as a performance test but as a deliberate counter to AF447-pattern skill decay.
7. **Maintain explicit "knock-it-off" and "unable" discipline.** Train both calls upward and downward in the IP/SP cockpit. Practice them. Reward their use, especially when the call turned out to be cautious-conservative. Never punish the call itself.

## Caveats

- **Smolensk investigation.** The MAK (Russian) and KBWLLP (Polish) findings concur that pilot error was the proximate cause, but they diverge on the role of Russian ATC information, the integrity of evidence handling, and the weight given to "psychological pressure" from senior passengers. A subsequent Polish re-investigation commission (under the post-2015 government) advanced explosion hypotheses that the original Polish commission and most international structural analysts reject. The summary in §6.2 reflects the consensus findings of the original MAK and KBWLLP reports.
- **Czar 52 supervisory chain.** Some details of the supervisory pattern in 1991–1994 (verbal reprimands without formal action, the precise content of McGeehan's conversations with wing leadership) are drawn from the AIB report and from secondary scholarly treatments (Kern 1995; Albany Scholars Archive). Direct CVR-equivalent evidence of the in-flight cockpit dynamic is limited because the B-52 was not required to carry a cockpit voice recorder at the time of the mishap; an unverified anecdote about a pocket cassette recording surfaced in online forums but is not part of the AIB record.
- **Colgan 3407 fatigue finding.** NTSB Chair Hersman called explicit attention to fatigue, but the full Board adopted language that fatigue's contribution "cannot be conclusively determined." The first officer's commute and the captain's overnight at Newark are documented; the precise causal weight is not. Squadron training should treat the case as a fatigue-and-training case, not a pure-fatigue case.
- **Air France 447 stall recognition.** The BEA noted that the possibility the crew did not perceive the stall warning aurally could not be ruled out; this is one of several explanations the report offered for the sustained nose-up inputs. The CRM degradation finding is robust; the precise cognitive mechanism is less so.
- **Open-source military sources.** Many militarily relevant fatigue, mishap, and CRM patterns are documented in classified or releasable-only-to-the-service material. The accidents cited here are the publicly available anchors; squadron-specific patterns may differ and should be reviewed with the squadron safety office.
- **Forward trends and projections.** Statements about ALIAS/MATRIX integration timelines, FRMS biomarker integration, and AI co-pilot adoption are based on programs and announcements current through late 2024 / early 2025. Specific 2025–2026 milestone completion status was not verifiable in open sources at the time of writing and should be tracked separately by the squadron safety officer.
